1. Problem Statement and Context Border checkpoints must decide within seconds whether a traveller's documents are genuine, current and actually belong to the person presenting them. The threats have grown beyond crude forgery. Attackers now use forged or digitally altered passports and visas, expired or reused documents, impersonation with genuine documents, and facial images that are morphed or synthetically generated. A morphed photograph is especially difficult because it can resemble two different people, so a document can pass a face comparison for both of them. Current verification relies on manual inspection and several disconnected checks: a visual examination, a separate MRZ read, and a separate database lookup. This has four consequences: - Time and congestion. Each additional check adds seconds per traveller, and queues build at peak hours. - Inconsistency. Outcomes depend on the experience, fatigue and attention of the officer on duty. - No explanation. Where automated tools exist, they tend to return a pass or fail verdict without showing what was wrong or where. - No verifiable record. There is rarely a tamper-evident account of what the system showed the officer and what the officer decided, which weakens accountability in disputed cases. Any solution must also handle sensitive identity and biometric data, so the design cannot solve the fraud problem by creating a new privacy problem. 2. Proposed Solution DRISHTI is a portable decision-support system that gives checkpoint personnel a single screening workflow covering document data validation, forensic analysis, biometric verification, structured risk scoring and tamper-evident record keeping. It does not make the final decision. It detects inconsistencies, explains them with evidence located on the document, and passes the decision to the officer. Every screening event is recorded on a permissioned ledger. Design principles - Inexpensive checks run first, and deep models run on demand. - Every flag carries evidence and a location on the document. - Only hashes reach the ledger, never identity data. - The officer makes the final decision. What is different about this approach - Adaptive-depth cascade with randomised audits. Deterministic checks run first and heavy models run by risk. A random sample of low-risk documents still receives deep analysis, so an adversary cannot learn exactly what passes the light checks. - Localised, weighted evidence. Each inconsistency is a flag with a weight and a region on the document, rather than a single fake-or-real label. - Cross-document verification. The passport, visa and ID are checked against each other, which single-document inspection misses. - Verifier-checked explanations. A self-hosted language model writes the officer summary, and a code-based verifier rejects any summary containing a value that is not in the flag data. - Ledger-anchored audit trail including officer overrides. Merkle-batched hashes make both automated results and human decisions tamper-evident. 3. End-to-End Workflow An officer scans or uploads a passport, visa or ID at the checkpoint. The document then passes through a quality gate and a three-tier cascade. Modules within a tier can run concurrently, but the tiers themselves are sequential, so the computationally heavy Tier 2 models only run when the earlier tiers justify them. Quality gate The image is cropped and aligned and checked for blur and glare. An unusable image is returned with a retake prompt, so poor input never reaches the analysis stages. Tier 0: Deterministic gate (target under 0.3 s) - MRZ parsing and ICAO Doc 9303 check-digit validation - Date, format and expiry rules - Watchlist, stolen-document and document-number history lookup - A hard failure, such as a broken MRZ checksum or a blacklist match, exits directly to High Risk with the reason recorded. Tier 1: Light analysis (target 0.5 to 1 s) - OCR-to-MRZ field comparison - Barcode/QR decoding compared with printed fields - Template alignment of layout, fonts and text positions - Face detection, alignment and embedding, matched against a live capture with presentation-attack detection Tier 1 produces a partial score R1. Escalation rule The document proceeds to Tier 2 if R1 falls in the ambiguous band, if the case matches a high-risk profile, if it is drawn for a random audit, or if the checkpoint is running in full-depth mode. Otherwise it goes directly to the risk engine. Tier 2: Deep analysis (target 1 to 2 s, GPU) - Tamper localisation network - Error-level analysis, noise-residual and copy-move forensics - Stamp analysis - Morph and deepfake classifiers - Anomaly detector for forgery types not seen in training Risk engine, officer decision and audit The risk engine combines all flags into a score R and a band (Low Risk, Review or High Risk). The dashboard shows the flags, their locations and a short summary. The officer chooses to allow entry, send the traveller for further inspection, or reject entry, and may add a remark. The result, the officer's action and the model and ruleset versions are hashed and recorded on the ledger. The latency figures above are design targets and will be reported as measurements once evaluated. Worked example A: crude alteration caught at Tier 0 A passport image passes the quality gate. The MRZ is re-read at high OCR confidence, and the date-of-birth check digit is expected to be 4 but is found to be 7. A hard rule for MRZ integrity failure fires, and the case exits to High Risk in under one second. The officer sees the date-of-birth field highlighted with the note "DOB check digit mismatch". Worked example B: careful forgery that needs Tier 2 The checksums are valid, the dates are plausible and there is no watchlist hit, so Tier 0 is clean. At Tier 1 the OCR matches the MRZ and the layout is within tolerance, but the face similarity to the live capture is 0.41 against a match threshold of 0.55 and the photo boundary looks irregular. R1 falls in the ambiguous band and the case is routed to Tier 2. There the tamper heat-map concentrates on the photo region and the morph score is high. The total R exceeds the high threshold, and the officer sees the photo region highlighted with three stated reasons. The similarity and threshold values here are illustrative. 4. Module Details 4.1 OCR and MRZ extraction Visible text and the machine-readable zone are extracted with open-source OCR (Tesseract and EasyOCR). The MRZ is parsed according to ICAO Doc 9303 for the relevant document format, and the check digits for the document number, date of birth, date of expiry and the composite are recomputed using the standard 7-3-1 weighting. Where OCR confidence is low, the field is re-read or a fallback engine is used before any flag is raised, so that a misread character is not reported as a forgery. 4.2 Rule-based validation Rules check expiry and validity windows, logical consistency of dates (for example, issue date before expiry date and a plausible date of birth), field formats, issuing-state and authority information, and document-number lookups against watchlists and stolen-document lists. These checks are deterministic, cheap and fully explainable, which is why they sit in Tier 0. 4.3 Template, layout and barcode analysis The document is aligned to a reference template for its type, and the positions, fonts and styles of fields are compared with tolerance. Barcodes and QR codes are decoded and compared with printed fields. Security patterns and watermarks are checked where they are visible in the captured image. Templates are added per document type, so the same framework serves different agencies and documents. 4.4 Document forensics A tamper localisation network, following the tampered-text-detection approach and data of DocTamper, predicts which regions have been edited. It is combined with error-level analysis, noise-residual analysis and copy-move detection, which look for inconsistencies in compression and sensor noise, and with stamp analysis against known stamp templates. The output is a heat-map over the document that becomes the highlighted region shown to the officer. An anomaly detector adds coverage for forgery styles that were not present in training data. 4.5 Face verification and presentation-attack detection The face on the document is detected, aligned and embedded using ArcFace-style embeddings (InsightFace or FaceNet-class models) and compared with a live capture from the checkpoint camera. Liveness and presentation-attack checks are applied to the live capture, following the testing and reporting framework of ISO/IEC 30107-3, and optional depth-camera data can be used as an additional signal. The match threshold is tuned on held-out data and reported with false-accept and false-reject rates. 4.6 Morph and deepfake detection Classifiers are applied to the document photograph to detect morphing and synthetic manipulation, using ensembles rather than a single model to reduce sensitivity to any one attack type. Detection from a single document image is a hard problem, so the live-versus-document face comparison is treated as a complementary signal, and the evaluation follows the NIST FATE MORPH approach: attacks missed versus genuine photographs wrongly flagged. 4.7 Cross-document and visa checks - Names compared across documents with transliteration tolerance - Passport number on the visa matched to the passport - Validity windows checked for consistency - Same face across documents - For visas, MRZ content compared with the visible printed text, to detect discrepancies between machine-readable and human-readable data 4.8 History layer If the same document or identity has been screened before, verified and audited outcomes are brought in as a historical signal. The signal is bounded, so it can only move the score by a limited amount, and it decays with time, so an old outcome counts for less than a recent one. Only verified outcomes are used, and current evidence always remains the primary basis for the decision. This gives repeat cases continuity without creating a permanent identity-level suspicion. The exact bound and decay rate are calibration parameters, set during validation. 5. Risk Engine and Explainability The risk score is R = Sum(wi * Fi) + hard rules, where Fi is the value of evidence flag i and wi is its weight. The historical signal from Section 4.8 enters as a bounded term. Hard rules, such as a broken MRZ checksum or a blacklist hit, override the weighted sum and force High Risk. Flags Each detector and rule emits a structured flag. For example, a checksum failure is recorded as {"flag": "MRZ_DOB_CHECKDIGIT", "expected": 4, "found": 7, "weight": "high"}. Flags also carry a confidence value and, where relevant, the region of the document they refer to. Bands and calibration R is mapped to Low Risk, Review and High Risk. Initial weights come from expert rules and are refined on labelled validation data. Thresholds are chosen against a target false-alert rate for genuine travellers and a target catch rate for forged documents, and are versioned so that every change to policy is recorded. The Review band exists so that borderline cases go to a human reviewer instead of being rejected automatically. Officer-facing explanation - The dashboard shows every fired flag, its weight, its location as an overlay on the document, and the reason. - A self-hosted language model turns the structured flags into a two-to-three-sentence summary with one line per flag. - A code-based verifier checks that every number, field name and date in the summary appears in the flag data, and rejects the summary otherwise. - The model narrates and never decides, and because it is self-hosted, no data leaves the agency's environment. 6. Permissioned Blockchain Audit Layer What is stored where Passport images, facial data, biometric templates and full OCR records stay in encrypted off-chain storage under role-based access control. The ledger holds only: - SHA-256 hashes of documents and screening records - Audit ID and timestamp - Risk score and screening outcome - Model and ruleset versions - Status transitions and officer actions, including overrides Merkle batching Screening events are hashed and grouped into batches, and the Merkle root of each batch is written to the ledger. This keeps ledger writes manageable across many checkpoints, while any single event can still be proven with a Merkle inclusion proof. Hashes are computed over salted records, so that low-entropy fields cannot be guessed from the ledger contents. Why permissioned A permissioned network such as Hyperledger Fabric allows only authorised agencies to participate, uses managed identities for signing events, and supports endorsement policies, for example requiring more than one agency to endorse certain events. It has no per-transaction fees, and it does not expose screening timing or volume to anonymous outside nodes, which a public chain would. Fabric is the target architecture for the audit layer. Verification To check a record, an agency retrieves it from the off-chain store, recomputes its hash, and compares it with the anchored Merkle root using the inclusion proof. A mismatch shows that the record or its document state has been altered. Officer overrides are ledgered in the same way, so the trail covers human decisions as well as automated ones, and it records exactly what the system showed the officer. The ledger is tamper-evident: it detects alteration, and it is not intended to prevent someone with storage access from attempting it. 7. System Architecture and Technology Stack The system is a modular, API-first architecture in six layers: - Input layer. Documents (passport, visa, ID, permit), live capture for verification and anti-spoofing, and optional future hardware such as document scanners, cameras and RFID/NFC readers for ePassport chips. - Application layer. A React and Tailwind CSS officer dashboard for uploading or scanning, viewing real-time analysis, inspecting flagged regions and reasons, checking history and recording the final decision, connected to the backend over HTTPS REST. - Backend layer. FastAPI for orchestration, with a request handler (authentication and validation), a task orchestrator that manages the analysis pipeline, a result aggregator (flag scoring and the risk engine) and a response builder. - ML and analysis layer. OCR and MRZ extraction (Tesseract, OpenCV, EasyOCR), a custom rules engine, document forensics (OpenCV, PyTorch), face verification (InsightFace, FaceNet), morph and deepfake detection (PyTorch, custom CNN models), and cross-document checks with fuzzy matching. - Data and storage layer. PostgreSQL for operational data, encrypted S3-compatible object storage (AWS S3 or MinIO) for images, biometric data and evidence, and Redis for caching and the task queue. - Blockchain layer. SHA-256 hashing, Merkle batching and a permissioned Hyperledger Fabric ledger. Services are containerised with Docker. The API-first design lets watchlist and stolen-document connectors be mocked now and replaced with live integrations later. 8. Threats, Challenges and Mitigations Challenge: Poor image quality at counters Mitigation: Quality gate with a retake prompt, so poor input is not analysed. Challenge: False alarms on genuine travellers Mitigation: Calibrated thresholds and a Review band instead of automatic rejection; low-confidence OCR is re-read before flagging. Challenge: New, unseen forgery types Mitigation: Layered checks, anomaly detection and periodic retraining. Challenge: Attacks on the models and morph or deepfake drift Mitigation: Ensembles, non-ML deterministic checks, randomised deep audits and red-team tests. Challenge: Privacy of identity data Mitigation: Encryption, role-based access, hashes-only ledger, self-hosted language model and human review. Challenge: Bias in face matching Mitigation: Per-group error evaluation and human review of adverse outcomes. Challenge: Integration with agency databases Mitigation: API-first design with mock connectors now and live connectors later. Adversary model The design assumes adaptive adversaries who probe the system and tailor forgeries to it. Several independent layers, from checksums to forensics to face checks, must all be defeated at once. Randomised deep audits prevent an attacker from learning a fixed pass condition at Tier 1. Because the deterministic core does not depend on machine learning, it cannot be fooled by adversarial image perturbations alone. 9. Scalability and Deployment Load model - Peak load: lambda_peak = (checkpoints x documents per day x peak factor) / operating seconds - GPU need: GPUs = lambda_peak x (share reaching Tier 2) x (GPU-seconds per Tier 2 document) / target utilisation Illustration, with all values assumed: 50 checkpoints at 20,000 documents per day is about 11.6 documents per second on average, and with an assumed 3x peak factor about 35 documents per second. Because only a fraction of documents reach Tier 2, the GPU requirement is a fraction of what analysing every document at full depth would need. Scaling levers - A queue between capture and analysis, so peaks wait instead of failing. - Stateless workers, so CPU workers can be added horizontally for OCR and rules. - A batched GPU inference service shared by all checkpoints. - Edge and central split: Tiers 0 and 1 run at the checkpoint for a fast preliminary verdict and tolerance of poor connectivity, while Tier 2 and the ledger run centrally. - Merkle-batched anchoring, with one ledger write per batch. 10. Validation and Evaluation Plan Real passports are not needed for development. The team uses public specimen and synthetic datasets (MIDV-500, MIDV-2020, DocTamper and SIDTD) together with published morph generators, and calibrates on labelled validation data separate from the held-out test data. The deterministic core follows the published ICAO Doc 9303 specification, and the OCR, face and forensics components have mature open-source implementations. Module: Extraction and rules Metrics: Field-level accuracy, plus deliberately broken cases that must all be caught. Module: Tamper detection Metrics: Catch rate, false-alert rate and how well the flagged area matches the real edit. Module: Face verification Metrics: Wrong-person-accepted and right-person-rejected rates, tuned on held-out data and reported per demographic group. Module: Morph and fake photos Metrics: Attacks missed versus genuine photos wrongly flagged, following the NIST FATE MORPH evaluation. Module: Speed and load Metrics: Typical and worst-case time per document, and throughput under simultaneous load. These are metrics the team will report on held-out data. No accuracy figures are claimed in this document. 11. Responsible Use - A person makes the final call. The system scores and explains, and officers decide, especially for adverse outcomes. - Data is protected. Documents and faces are encrypted, the ledger holds hashes only, and retention is configurable to the agency's legal requirements. India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, and any exemptions applying to government bodies, are for the agency's counsel to interpret. - Fairness is tested. Face-matching errors are measured per demographic group, since NIST's demographic-effects study (NISTIR 8280) found that error rates differ between groups in many algorithms. - Accountability. Thresholds and weights are versioned, and the ledger records what the system showed and what the officer decided. 12. Impact and Benefits - Border and immigration officers. They receive a scored verdict with the flagged part of the document highlighted and the reason in plain language, so a check that takes minutes by eye becomes seconds of reading. - Genuine travellers. Documents that pass cleanly move quickly, and borderline cases go to a person for review instead of being rejected automatically. - Agencies and supervisors. Every checkpoint applies the same checks and thresholds, so decisions no longer depend on the officer or shift, and policy changes are versioned. - Investigators and analysts. Each screening leaves a tamper-evident record, and repeat faces and reused document numbers can be linked across checkpoints. - Security and society. Layered checks are harder to defeat than single-document inspection, which supports action against identity fraud, human trafficking and cross-border crime while keeping legitimate travel moving. The approach aligns with the objectives of IVFRT modernization. - Economy. Software checks scale with volume, so cost per checkpoint falls as traffic grows, and the same framework serves other agencies by adding document templates. 13. Roadmap - Core platform: upload, OCR and MRZ extraction, rule validation, face matching, risk engine, officer dashboard and hashed audit log. - Deeper detection: tamper localisation, morph and deepfake classifiers, cross-document checks and multiple-identity search. - Integration: live watchlist and stolen-document connectors, ePassport chip verification, permissioned ledger deployment and a pilot. 14.
